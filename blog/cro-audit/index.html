Most websites do not have a traffic problem. They have a leak. Visitors arrive from search, ads and email, then quietly leave at a pricing page, a form or a checkout step that nobody on the team has looked at in months. A CRO audit finds those leaks with data instead of opinions, so you fix the pages that cost you the most money first.

A CRO audit (conversion rate optimization audit) is a structured review of your analytics, user behavior, page design, forms, checkout and site speed to find where visitors drop out of your funnel and why. It ends with a prioritized list of fixes and a small set of A/B tests, each with a hypothesis, a primary metric and a sample size you can realistically reach.

How to use this guide: it is written as a five-day plan a marketer can run alone or with one developer. Every platform fact and statistic links to the source we checked (Google Analytics Help, web.dev, Baymard Institute and Nielsen Norman Group). The funnel numbers in the figures are illustrative examples, not client data.

What Is a CRO Audit?

A CRO audit answers three questions in order: where do people drop out, why do they drop out, and which fix is worth doing first. It is different from a redesign. A redesign changes everything at once, so you cannot tell which change helped. An audit isolates specific problems and turns them into fixes or tests you can measure one at a time.

A good audit combines two kinds of evidence. Quantitative data (GA4 funnels, segments, speed data) tells you where the problem is. Qualitative data (heatmaps, session recordings, surveys, usability sessions) tells you why. Relying on only one of them is the most common reason audits produce long lists of guesses instead of fixes.

When Should You Run a CRO Audit?

  • Traffic is rising but leads or sales are flat. More visitors with the same output usually means the new traffic does not match the page, or the page has friction.
  • Before scaling paid media. Every dollar you add to Google Ads management buys more of the same conversion rate, so fix the landing page first.
  • After a redesign, replatform or new checkout. Launches often break tracking, forms or mobile layouts in ways nobody notices for weeks.
  • On a schedule. A light audit every quarter and a full audit once a year keeps small problems from compounding.

The One-Week CRO Audit Plan

You can run a useful audit in five working days if you keep the scope to your top two or three converting journeys (for example, the main lead form and the product-to-checkout path). Here is how the week breaks down.

Step 1: Set Goals and Baselines in GA4

Start by agreeing on what a conversion is. In GA4, the actions that matter to your business are called key events. Google's help page on key events describes them as measuring "actions that are important to your business," and you mark an existing event as a key event by clicking the star next to it under Admin, Data display, Events. Two details catch people out: marking an event only affects data from that point forward, and Google Ads conversions created from key events do not appear in standard GA4 reports.

Check tracking before you trust it

Submit every form and complete a test purchase yourself, then confirm each event fires once (not twice) in GA4's DebugView or Realtime report. Duplicate purchase events, thank-you pages that load without a submission and forms that post to a third-party tool without firing an event are the usual culprits. If tracking is broken, fixing it is the first finding of the audit.

Record your baselines

For the last 90 days, record conversion rate, conversions and revenue or lead volume, split by device (mobile, desktop) and by channel (organic, paid, email, referral). Segments matter because a sitewide average hides problems: a checkout that converts well on desktop can be failing on mobile, and a landing page that works for branded search can fail for cold paid traffic.

Step 2: Find Funnel Drop-Off With Funnel Explorations

Next, map each journey as a funnel. In GA4, go to Explore and choose Funnel exploration. According to Google's funnel exploration documentation, "You can define up to 10 steps in the funnel," and each step can be based on events or dimensions, though "You can't define funnel steps based on metrics."

Three settings change what the report tells you:

  • Open or closed funnel. "In an open funnel, users can enter the funnel in any step." Use a closed funnel when you want to see the full path from the first step; use an open funnel when people often land mid-journey, such as directly on a product page from Google Shopping.
  • Elapsed time. Turning on "Show elapsed time" displays the average time between steps. A long gap between "begin checkout" and "add payment info" often signals confusion or a surprise cost.
  • Next action. Choose a dimension such as page path to see the "Top 5 Next Actions" after each step. If people leave checkout for your returns or shipping page, the checkout is not answering that question.

Read the funnel in relative terms. In the example above, the landing page loses more people in absolute numbers, but the step from product view to add-to-cart loses 69% of the people who were already interested. That is the step where heatmaps, recordings and a heuristic review are most likely to pay off. Break the same funnel down by device and channel before you move on: a drop that only appears on mobile is a design or speed problem, while a drop that only appears for one traffic source is usually a message-match problem.

Step 3: Watch Real Behavior With Heatmaps, Recordings and Surveys

Numbers tell you which step leaks. Qualitative research tells you why. On day three, focus only on the pages behind your biggest drop-offs.

Heatmaps

Click and scroll heatmaps show what people try to interact with and how far they get. Look for clicks on elements that are not links (people expect them to be), primary calls to action below the point most mobile users reach, and heavy attention on content that does not lead anywhere. Review mobile and desktop heatmaps separately, since the layouts differ.

Session recordings

Filter recordings to sessions that reached your leaking step and then left. Watch 20 to 30 of them and log every pattern: rage clicks, repeated field errors, scrolling up and down looking for a price, or a sticky banner covering the button. Patterns that repeat across several sessions become findings; one-off oddities do not.

On-page surveys and quick usability tests

Ask one question at the point of hesitation, such as "Is anything stopping you from booking today?" on a pricing page or an exit survey on checkout. Then run a few short moderated sessions. Nielsen Norman Group's research on testing with five users found that a first study "with five participants has found 85% of the usability problems," which makes small, repeated rounds of testing far better value than one large study.

Step 4: Run a Heuristic Landing Page Audit

A heuristic review is an expert walk-through of each key page against established usability principles. Nielsen Norman Group's 10 usability heuristics, first published by Jakob Nielsen in 1994, are still the most useful frame. For a landing page audit, translate the most relevant ones into marketing questions:

  • Match between the system and the real world: does the headline use the words your customers and your ads use, or internal jargon?
  • Visibility of system status: after someone submits a form or adds to cart, is it obvious what happened and what comes next?
  • Error prevention: do fields accept common formats for phone numbers and ZIP codes, or reject them and make people retype?
  • Recognition rather than recall: are price, plan details and key benefits visible at the decision point, or do people have to remember them from another page?
  • Aesthetic and minimalist design: does anything compete with the primary call to action, such as carousels, multiple equal-weight buttons or pop-ups?

Add three conversion-specific checks to the usability ones: message match with the ad, email or search query that brought the visitor; trust signals near the call to action (reviews, guarantees, security, clear contact details); and a single, specific next step. For ecommerce category and product pages, these checks overlap heavily with technical and content work, which is where our ecommerce SEO service often starts.

Step 5: Audit Forms and Checkout Friction

Forms and checkouts are where intent turns into revenue, and they are often the cheapest place to win. Baymard Institute's cart abandonment research puts the documented average cart abandonment rate at 70.22%, based on 50 studies. Among US shoppers who abandoned during checkout (excluding those who were just browsing), the top reasons were:

Reason for abandoning checkoutShare of US shoppersWhat to check in your audit
Extra costs too high (shipping, tax, fees)40%Show shipping and fees on the product or cart page, before checkout
Delivery was too slow20%Show delivery dates, not just "standard shipping"
Didn't trust the site with card information19%Visible security cues, recognizable payment options, clear contact details
Site wanted me to create an account18%Offer guest checkout and make it the obvious path
Too long or complicated checkout17%Count fields and steps; remove anything optional
Website had errors or crashed17%Test every payment method on real mobile devices

Field count is the most measurable fix. Baymard reports that the average US checkout displays 23.48 form elements by default, while its testing suggests an ideal flow needs 12 to 14 form elements (7 to 8 fields). The same research estimates that fixing solvable checkout usability issues could lift conversion by 35.26% for an average large ecommerce site. Treat that as Baymard's estimate for large stores, not a promise for yours, but it shows how much room checkouts usually have.

For lead-generation sites, apply the same logic to forms: ask only for what sales needs on the first contact, mark optional fields clearly, use inline validation, and send an instant confirmation. If leads go cold after the form, the problem may be follow-up speed rather than the page, which is where marketing automation that routes and answers new leads immediately can matter as much as the form itself.

Step 6: Check Speed and Core Web Vitals

Slow, jumpy pages hurt conversion at every step. Google's Web Vitals guidance on web.dev sets the "good" thresholds as Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) within 2.5 seconds, Interaction to Next Paint (INP) of 200 milliseconds or less, and Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) of 0.1 or less, measured at the 75th percentile of page loads and segmented across mobile and desktop.

In a CRO audit, check these on the specific pages in your leaking funnel steps, not just the homepage. Use field data from the Chrome UX Report (in PageSpeed Insights or Search Console) where available, because lab tests on a fast office connection rarely match what mobile visitors experience. Pay particular attention to:

  • LCP on landing pages: oversized hero images and render-blocking scripts delay the first meaningful view.
  • INP on forms and checkout: heavy third-party scripts make buttons and fields feel unresponsive, which reads as "broken" to a buyer.
  • CLS near calls to action: late-loading banners and cookie notices that push the button down cause mis-taps.

For the diagnosis and fixes themselves, our guide to advanced Core Web Vitals walks through LCP, INP and CLS step by step.

Step 7: Prioritize Findings With ICE or PIE

By day five you will have more findings than you can act on. A scoring model keeps the decision honest. The two most common are:

  • ICE: score each finding 1 to 10 for Impact (how much it could move the key event), Confidence (how strong your evidence is) and Ease (how quickly it can be shipped), then average or multiply the scores.
  • PIE: score Potential (how much the page can improve), Importance (how much valuable traffic it gets) and Ease. PIE works well for ranking pages; ICE works well for ranking individual ideas.

Base the Confidence score on evidence, not enthusiasm. A finding seen in the funnel data, in recordings and in survey answers deserves a higher score than one idea from a single stakeholder. Then split the backlog in two: clear bugs and obvious friction (a broken mobile button, a hidden shipping cost) should simply be fixed, while changes where reasonable people disagree (a new headline, a shorter page, a different offer) should be tested.

Step 8: Design Tests That Can Actually Reach a Result

Every A/B test brief needs four things: a hypothesis tied to a finding ("Because recordings show mobile users scrolling back up to find the price, showing the price next to the button will increase add-to-cart rate"), one primary metric, the audience and pages included, and a sample size calculated before launch.

Sample size is where most small-business tests fail. Calculators such as Evan Miller's A/B test sample size calculator take your baseline conversion rate, the minimum detectable effect, statistical power and significance level. Using the standard two-proportion formula at 95% significance and 80% power, our own calculation for a page converting at 3% gives these rough requirements:

Baseline conversion rateRelative lift to detectVisitors needed per variation (approx.)
3%20% (3.0% to 3.6%)About 13,900
3%10% (3.0% to 3.3%)About 53,200
5%20% (5.0% to 6.0%)About 8,200

If a page cannot reach those numbers in four to six weeks, do not run a split test on it. Test bolder changes that could plausibly produce a larger effect, test on a higher-traffic step of the funnel, or ship the change and monitor the before-and-after trend with the caveat that seasonality and campaigns can muddy it. Set the run time in advance, run for whole weeks so weekday and weekend behavior are both included, and do not stop a test early because the dashboard looks good on day three.

Common CRO Audit Mistakes

  • Auditing before checking tracking. If key events double-count or miss form submissions, every conclusion that follows is wrong.
  • Copying a competitor's page. You see their design, not their test results, traffic mix or margins.
  • Looking only at sitewide averages. Most real problems live in one device, one channel or one step.
  • Testing everything. Bugs should be fixed, not tested, and low-traffic pages cannot support split tests.
  • Treating the audit as a one-off. Pages, offers and traffic change. Re-run the light version every quarter and feed results into your wider small business marketing strategy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a CRO audit?

A CRO audit is a structured review of your analytics, user behavior, page design, forms, checkout and site speed that finds where visitors drop out of your funnel and why. It ends with a prioritized list of fixes and a few A/B tests with clear hypotheses and sample sizes.

How long does a CRO audit take?

A focused audit of two or three key journeys can be done in about five working days: two days for goals, tracking and funnel analysis, two days for qualitative research and page reviews, and one day for prioritization and test design. Larger sites with many templates or markets take longer.

What tools do I need for a CRO audit?

At minimum you need Google Analytics 4 with working key events, a heatmap and session recording tool, a way to run a short on-page survey, and PageSpeed Insights or Search Console for Core Web Vitals. An A/B testing tool is only needed once you have tests with enough traffic to reach a result.

How do I find funnel drop-off in GA4?

Go to Explore, choose Funnel exploration and add up to 10 steps based on events or dimensions, such as page view, add to cart, begin checkout and purchase. Use an open funnel if people often enter mid-journey, turn on elapsed time to spot slow steps, and break the funnel down by device and channel.

What is the difference between ICE and PIE scoring?

ICE scores each idea for Impact, Confidence and Ease, which suits ranking individual fixes. PIE scores Potential, Importance and Ease, which suits ranking which pages to work on first. Both are simple frameworks; what matters most is basing the scores on evidence from your audit rather than opinion.

How much traffic do I need for A/B testing?

It depends on your baseline conversion rate and the lift you want to detect. At 95% significance and 80% power, a page converting at 3% needs roughly 13,900 visitors per variation to detect a 20% relative lift, and about 53,200 to detect a 10% lift. Low-traffic pages are better served by fixes and bolder changes than by split tests.

Should I do a CRO audit myself or hire someone?

A marketer with GA4 access and one developer can run the process in this guide. Outside help is worth it when tracking is unreliable, when the team is too close to the pages to see friction, or when you need someone to run tests and implement changes across analytics, paid media and automation.

Where to Start

Pick your single most valuable journey, verify its tracking today and build the funnel in GA4. The biggest relative drop-off tells you where to spend the rest of the week. Fix the obvious friction, test the debatable changes and re-check the numbers after a full month.
