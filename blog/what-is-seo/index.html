What is SEO? SEO (search engine optimization) is the work of making your website easy for search engines to find, understand and trust, so it shows up when people search for what you sell. If you run a business, SEO decides whether a customer who searches "emergency plumber near me" or "best payroll software for restaurants" finds you or a competitor. This guide explains what SEO means, how Google actually crawls, indexes and ranks pages, the four types of SEO, how it compares with paid ads, and what changes now that Google answers many questions with AI.

SEO, short for search engine optimization, is the practice of improving a website so search engines can crawl it, understand it and rank it for relevant searches, without paying for the placement. It covers technical health (can Google access and index your pages), on-page content (does each page answer the search), off-page signals (do other sites and customers vouch for you) and, for businesses that serve an area, local SEO. The goal is more qualified visitors from organic results, maps and AI answers.

How this guide was written: every claim about how Google works comes from Google's own documentation, linked in place. Where we share our own view as practitioners, we say so.

What Is SEO? The Plain-English Meaning

Google's own SEO Starter Guide describes SEO as being "about helping search engines understand your content, and helping users find your site and make a decision about whether they should visit your site through a search engine." That is a useful definition because it has two halves. One half is for the machine (can Google read and classify your page). The other half is for the person (does your listing look like the right answer, and does the page deliver once they click).

In practice, the SEO meaning most business owners care about is simple: showing up in the unpaid (organic) results when your customers search. Those include the classic blue links, the local map pack, image and video results and, more and more, AI-generated summaries that link to sources.

Three things SEO is not:

  • It is not buying a ranking. Google states that it "doesn't accept payment to crawl a site more frequently, or rank it higher. If anyone tells you otherwise, they're wrong."
  • It is not a one-time setup. Your competitors, your customers' questions and Google's systems all change, so SEO is ongoing maintenance and improvement.
  • It is not tricking Google. Tactics like keyword stuffing and buying links break Google's spam policies and can get pages demoted or removed.

How Does SEO Work? Crawling, Indexing and Ranking

To understand how SEO works, you need to understand how a search engine works. Google's in-depth guide to how Google Search works says plainly that Search "works in three stages, and not all pages make it through each stage": crawling, indexing and serving search results. SEO is largely the job of making sure your important pages survive all three.

Stage 1: Crawling

There is no central registry of web pages, so Google has to discover them. Google explains that it finds new pages mostly by following links from pages it already knows and from sitemaps that site owners submit. A program called Googlebot then fetches the page and renders it, running JavaScript "using a recent version of Chrome," much like your browser does. Pages can fail here if robots.txt blocks them, if the server errors out or if content sits behind a login.

What SEO does at this stage: clean internal linking so every important page is reachable, an XML sitemap, a robots.txt that does not block what you want found, and a reliable server.

Stage 2: Indexing

After crawling, Google tries to understand what the page is about by analyzing its text, title element, images, video and other content. It also groups near-identical pages and picks one version, the canonical, to show in results. Google is clear that "indexing isn't guaranteed; not every page that Google processes will be indexed," and it lists low-quality content and noindex rules among the common reasons.

What SEO does at this stage: one clear URL per topic, descriptive titles and headings, important content in real text rather than locked inside images, and pages substantial enough to be worth storing.

Stage 3: Serving and ranking

When someone searches, Google looks through its index and returns "the results we believe are the highest quality and most relevant to the user's query." Google says relevance is shaped by hundreds of factors, including the searcher's location, language and device. Its own example: a search for "bicycle repair shops" shows different results in Paris than in Hong Kong. The type of result changes too. A local service query tends to trigger a map; a product query may trigger images or shopping results.

What SEO does at this stage: matching each page to what searchers actually want, making it the most useful answer available, and building the reputation signals (links, mentions, reviews) that tell Google other people trust you.

What Are the Main Types of SEO?

Each type of SEO fixes a different problem. Most businesses need all four; the mix depends on your site and market.

Technical SEO

Technical SEO is the foundation: making sure search engines can crawl, render and index your site efficiently. It covers site architecture, XML sitemaps, robots.txt, redirects, canonical tags, HTTPS, mobile usability, structured data and page speed. If you have ever had a page that "should rank" but never appears at all, the cause is often technical. Our guide to diagnosing and fixing Core Web Vitals goes deep on the speed side.

On-page SEO

On-page SEO is everything on the page itself: the topic you choose, the title and meta description, headings, the body copy, images, internal links and structured data. The core question is whether the page is the most helpful answer to the search that brings people to it. Google's guidance repeatedly points to "helpful, reliable, people-first content" rather than any formula of word counts or keyword density.

Off-page SEO

Off-page SEO is your reputation beyond your own website: links from other relevant sites, brand mentions, press coverage and reviews. Google notes that PageRank, which uses links, is "one of the fundamental algorithms at Google," while adding that it is only one of many signals. The safe way to build off-page strength is to earn it with things worth citing, such as original data, useful tools and genuine partnerships. Buying links for ranking purposes is listed as link spam.

Local SEO

Local SEO is for businesses that serve customers in a specific area, like dentists, HVAC contractors, law firms and restaurants. It centers on your Google Business Profile and how you appear in Google Maps and the local pack. According to Google Business Profile Help, local results are "mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity." Google breaks this down into relevance, distance and prominence, and says prominence draws on information like how many websites link to you and how many reviews you have. Google also states there is "no way to request or pay for a better local ranking." If reviews are your weak spot, see our guide on how to get more Google reviews, or our local SEO service.

Specialized types, such as ecommerce SEO or franchise SEO, are these four applied to a particular kind of site.

SEO vs PPC vs SEM: What Is the Difference?

These three terms get mixed up constantly, so here is the short version.

  • SEO earns placement in the unpaid (organic) results.
  • PPC (pay-per-click) buys placement in ad slots. Google Ads Help explains that with cost-per-click bidding "you pay for each click on your ads," and that CPC pricing "is sometimes known as pay-per-click (PPC)."
  • SEM (search engine marketing) is an umbrella term. Some people use it to mean paid search only; others use it for paid and organic search together. When someone says SEM, ask which they mean.
QuestionSEOPPC
What do you pay for?Content, technical work and expertise. No fee per click.Each click (on most search campaigns), plus management.
How fast does it work?Weeks to months, depending on the change and the competition.As soon as ads are approved and running.
What happens when you stop?Rankings often hold for a while, then fade as competitors improve.Traffic from ads stops.
Does one help the other?Google says "Advertising with Google won't have any effect on your site's presence in our search results." They share keyword and landing page insights, not rankings.

The honest answer to "which is better" is that it depends on timing. If you need leads next week, PPC is the faster lever. If you want a channel whose cost per lead tends to fall as your pages mature, SEO is the long game. Many businesses use paid search to test which keywords convert, then invest in SEO for the winners. What Does SEO Mean in the Age of AI Search?

Google now answers many searches with AI Overviews (a summary at the top of results) and AI Mode (a conversational search experience). Outside Google, tools like ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini answer questions and cite websites. This has led some people to say SEO is dead. Google's own documentation says the opposite.

Google's guide to AI features and your website states that "the best practices for SEO remain relevant for AI features in Google Search" and that "there are no additional requirements to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode, nor other special optimizations necessary." To be shown as a supporting link, a page "must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet." In other words, the crawl, index and rank pipeline above is still the entry ticket.

A few things from that same document are worth knowing:

  • Both AI Overviews and AI Mode "may use a 'query fan-out' technique," running several related searches across subtopics to build a response. Pages that clearly answer specific sub-questions have more chances to be pulled in.
  • Google says you do not need "new machine readable files, AI text files, or markup" and that there is "no special schema.org structured data that you need to add."
  • Traffic from AI features is counted inside the overall "Web" search type in Search Console, so you cannot isolate it there.

Where AI search does change the job is emphasis. Clear, quotable answers, consistent facts about your business across the web, and being mentioned by sources that AI tools trust all matter more than they used to. How Long Does SEO Take?

Here is Google's own wording from the SEO Starter Guide: "Some changes might take effect in a few hours, others could take several months. In general, you likely want to wait a few weeks to assess whether your work had beneficial effects in Google Search results." Google also cautions that "not all changes you make to your website will result in noticeable impact."

That range is wide because the timeline depends on what you are fixing and who you are competing against. From our own work, these factors move the timeline the most:

  • Fixing a blocker is fast. If a stray noindex tag or robots.txt rule was hiding important pages, results can recover once Google recrawls them.
  • New content takes longer. A new page has to be discovered, indexed and then earn its place against pages that have been around for years.
  • Competition sets the bar. Ranking for a local service in a small town is a different project from ranking nationally for a high-value software term.
  • Site history matters. A site with existing authority and clean technical health usually responds faster than a brand-new domain.

Be wary of anyone who promises a specific position by a specific date. Google's page on whether you need an SEO is blunt: "No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google." A good provider gives you realistic estimates, explains what they will change and shows you how they will measure progress.

Common SEO Myths, Checked Against Google's Own Guidance

Much of what circulates about SEO is outdated or wrong. Google's SEO Starter Guide addresses several myths directly:

MythWhat Google says
"You need the meta keywords tag."Google Search "doesn't use the keywords meta tag."
"Repeat your keyword as often as possible."Excessive repetition is tiring for users, and "keyword stuffing is against Google's spam policies."
"Pages need at least 2,000 words.""The length of the content alone doesn't matter for ranking purposes."
"Put keywords in your domain name."Keywords in the domain or URL path "have hardly any effect beyond appearing in breadcrumbs."
"Duplicate content gets you penalized."The same content under multiple URLs is inefficient but "not something that will cause a manual action." Copying other sites' content is a different matter.
"E-E-A-T is a ranking factor."Google's answer: "No, it's not." It describes qualities Google's systems try to reward, not a score you can optimize directly.

Can You Do SEO Yourself, or Do You Need Help?

Google's own advice is encouraging for small businesses: "If you run a small local business, you can probably do much of the work yourself," starting with the SEO Starter Guide. Many owners handle the basics well, such as claiming their Google Business Profile, writing clear service pages and asking happy customers for reviews.

Outside help tends to pay off when the site is large or technically complex, the market is competitive, you are planning a redesign (Google calls this "a great time to hire"), or nobody on your team has the hours. Google suggests asking providers for past work, whether they follow Google Search Essentials, and what results they expect on what timeline. Frequently Asked Questions

What is SEO in simple terms?

SEO, or search engine optimization, is the work of improving your website so search engines like Google can find it, understand it and show it to people searching for what you offer. It earns unpaid (organic) visibility in search results, maps and AI answers, rather than paying for each click like ads.

How does SEO work?

Google works in three stages: crawling (discovering and fetching pages), indexing (understanding and storing them) and serving (ranking the most relevant, helpful results for each search). SEO makes sure your important pages can be crawled and indexed, match what searchers want, and carry the trust signals, like links and reviews, that help them rank.

What are the main types of SEO?

The four main types are technical SEO (crawlability, indexing, speed and site structure), on-page SEO (content, titles, headings and internal links), off-page SEO (links, mentions and reputation) and local SEO (Google Business Profile and map results). Ecommerce and franchise SEO apply these same types to specific kinds of sites.

What is the difference between SEO and SEM?

SEO earns unpaid placement in organic results. SEM, or search engine marketing, is an umbrella term that many people use for paid search ads (PPC), and some use for paid and organic search together. Paid ads deliver traffic immediately but stop when the budget stops; SEO builds more slowly, and Google says advertising has no effect on organic rankings.

How long does SEO take to work?

Google's SEO Starter Guide says some changes "might take effect in a few hours, others could take several months," and recommends waiting a few weeks to assess results. Fixing a technical blocker can show results quickly, while new content in a competitive market usually takes months to earn strong rankings.

Is SEO still relevant with AI Overviews and ChatGPT?

Yes. Google states that SEO best practices "remain relevant" for AI Overviews and AI Mode, with no extra requirements, and a page must be indexed and eligible for a snippet to appear as a supporting link. AI search raises the value of clear answers and a consistent reputation across the web, but it builds on SEO rather than replacing it.
