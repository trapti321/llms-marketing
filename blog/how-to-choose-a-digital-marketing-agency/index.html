How to Choose a Digital Marketing Agency: A Vetting Process With Questions, a Scorecard and Red Flags

Most bad agency relationships are decided before the contract is signed. The business picks on a polished pitch, a low monthly fee or a promise that sounded too good to question, and finds out six months later that nobody agreed on what success meant, the ad account sits in the agency's name, and leaving means starting from zero. Knowing how to choose a digital marketing agency is less about finding the "best" agency and more about running a process that exposes weak ones early.

To choose a digital marketing agency, define the business outcome and budget first, build a long list from referrals and specialists, cut it with a quick proof check, ask every finalist the same questions, verify their case studies and references yourself, score them on one weighted scorecard, and only sign a contract that keeps your Google Ads, GA4, Google Business Profile and website in your name with clear deliverables and exit terms.

Where this guide comes from: we are an agency, so this is the process we would want a careful buyer to put us through. Platform facts come from Google's help documentation and the FTC, linked in place. Looking for names to vet? Start with our list of the top digital marketing companies in the US.

Step 1: Define Goals Before You Talk to Anyone

An agency can only be judged against a goal. Without one, each agency defines success in terms that suit its own services (rankings, return on ad spend, engagement), and you end up comparing three different promises instead of three routes to the same result.

Write a one-page brief before you contact anyone. It should answer five questions:

  • The outcome: more qualified leads, online sales or booked appointments. Pick one.
  • The number: the KPI that proves it (such as cost per qualified lead) and where you measure it today.
  • The budget: monthly agency fee and, separately, ad spend, for at least six months.
  • The channels you suspect matter: based on how customers find you. If unsure, our guide to which digital marketing services your business needs walks through this by business type, and it is the right step one.
  • What you will do internally: who approves content, who answers leads, who owns the CRM.

Send every agency the same brief. You will see quickly who read it and who sent a template.

Agency, In-House or Both?

Before you vet agencies, confirm that an agency is the right model. Google's own guidance on whether you need an SEO makes a point that applies to most marketing: "If you run a small local business, you can probably do much of the work yourself." The same page suggests learning the basics even if you hire, so you can tell when someone proposes a technique that is "not recommended or, worse, strongly discouraged."

An agency fits when you need several specialist skills (technical SEO, paid media, analytics) that one hire cannot cover, or you need to start fast. An in-house hire fits when the work is constant, product-heavy and big enough for a full-time role. Many growing businesses land on a hybrid: an internal marketer owns strategy and approvals while an agency handles specialist execution. Whatever you choose, the account ownership rules later in this guide apply equally to a freelancer.

Step 2: Build a Shortlist Worth Your Time

Start with a long list of eight to ten agencies from people who have already paid for the work: owners in similar businesses, your web developer, industry peers, and specialists who publish detailed work in your niche. Cold-email agencies go to the bottom, for reasons covered under red flags.

Then run a 15-minute proof check on each one, before any call:

  1. Does their own marketing work? An SEO agency that cannot be found for its own services is a signal.
  2. Are case studies specific? Look for a named client or at least a clear industry, the starting point, what they changed, the time frame and the metric. "We grew traffic 300%" with no baseline, date or business outcome tells you nothing.
  3. Who are the people? Real names, roles and visible work history. Anonymous teams hide who will run your account.
  4. Do they specialize in your situation? Specialization by industry or channel is not essential, but the agency should be able to explain why its experience fits your outcome. Law firms, for example, face advertising rules most agencies have never handled, which is why we wrote a separate guide on how to choose a law firm SEO company.

Most long lists shrink to three to five agencies after this check. That is the right number for discovery calls.

Step 3: Questions to Ask a Marketing Agency

Ask every finalist the same questions in the same order and take notes, so you can compare answers rather than impressions. Google's guide lists useful starting questions for SEO hires, including "What kind of results do you expect to see, and in what timeframe? How do you measure your success?" and "Will you share with me all the changes you make to my site?" Google also says a good SEO "should ask questions about what makes your business unique." If an agency asks you nothing, that is an answer too.

QuestionWhat a good answer sounds likeWarning sign

On the last question, Google now addresses it directly. Its hiring guide asks, of agencies selling "AEO" or "GEO" services, whether "their advice [is] aligned with Google Search's official guidance on optimizing for generative AI features." That is a fair test for any agency, including our own AI search visibility service.

Step 4: Verify Case Studies, Reviews and References

Everything an agency shows you in a pitch has been selected. Your job in this step is to check that the best examples are real and typical.

Case studies

  • Ask for the time frame, the starting baseline, what the agency did versus what the client did, and whether the result held after the engagement.
  • Ask to see one result in the source system on a screen share, such as GA4, Search Console or the ad account, with client details hidden if needed.
  • Check that the metric matters. A traffic increase that did not change leads or revenue is not the result you are buying.

Reviews

Read reviews on more than one platform and look for specifics: named staff, real problems and how they were fixed. Bursts of short, generic five-star reviews deserve a closer look, and the stakes are legal as well as practical. The FTC's final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials, announced on August 14, 2024, prohibits fake reviews (including AI-generated ones), buying positive or negative reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled review sites posing as independent, and fake social media indicators such as bot followers, and lets the agency seek civil penalties against knowing violators. An agency willing to fake its own reputation may offer to fake yours, and your business carries the risk. For the legitimate way to grow reviews, see our guide on how to get more Google reviews.

References

Google recommends you "ask past clients if they felt that this SEO provided useful service, was easy to work with, and produced positive results." Ask for two current clients and, if the agency will provide one, a former client. Useful questions for a reference: how long have you worked together, who handles your account, what went wrong and how was it handled, and would you hire them again for the same job?

Step 5: Score Every Agency on the Same Card

After the calls, score each finalist on the same criteria while your notes are fresh. Weight the criteria to match your brief. The weights below suit a typical small or midsize business buying lead generation. Rate each criterion from 1 to 5, multiply by the weight and add up the total.

CriterionWeightWhat a 5 looks like
Fit with your goal and channel20%Proposal is built around your brief and outcome
Proof you can verify20%Specific case studies shown in source data; references called back
Quality of first-call thinking15%Asked sharp questions, spotted a real issue in your setup
Who does the work15%Named, experienced people with reasonable workloads
Reporting tied to revenue10%KPIs from your GA4, CRM or ad account, with a baseline
Contract and account ownership10%You own every account; clear exit and handover
Pricing clarity10%Fee, scope and exclusions in writing

Two rules make the scorecard useful. Treat account ownership as a pass or fail gate as well as a score: an agency that will not put accounts in your name should not win on points. And if two agencies finish close together, pick the one whose first 90-day plan you understood best. Clarity before signing tends to predict clarity after.

Agency Pricing Models and What Each One Rewards

Prices vary too much by scope and market for a useful average, so compare the pricing model instead, because each one rewards different behavior. Our overview of how digital marketing agencies charge lists the models; here is what each one means for you as a buyer.

ModelWhat it rewardsAsk before signing
Monthly retainerSteady, ongoing work and continuityWhat ships each month, and what happens to unused hours or deliverables
Project feeDelivering a defined output (site build, audit, migration)What counts as done, how many revisions, and what change requests cost
Performance-basedResults the contract can countHow a lead or sale is defined, who tracks it, and what happens to lead quality
Percentage of ad spendManaging larger budgetsHow the agency will tell you if spend should go down

When you compare proposals, rewrite each one in the same format: monthly fee, ad spend (paid to the platform), software costs, one-time setup, minimum term and the deliverables you get each month. For performance pricing, insist that the definition of a qualified lead is written into the contract, or you may pay for form fills your sales team cannot use.

Step 6: Marketing Agency Contract Terms to Check

The contract is where most of the long-term risk sits. Before you sign, confirm these terms in writing.

Account ownership: Google Ads, GA4 and Google Business Profile

Create the accounts under your company's Google account and add the agency as users. The platforms are designed for this:

  • Google Ads: Google's help page on sharing access to your Google Ads account explains that after granting access "you can edit a user's access level, or you can always remove access later on." It also warns that with only one administrator "you may lose access to your tags if that user becomes unavailable," so keep an admin login inside your company. Our Google Ads management always runs in the client's own account.
  • GA4: per Google's guide to adding and managing Analytics users, you need the Administrator role to add or modify users, and only account-level Administrators can delete users. Keep Administrator at the account level yourself and give the agency the role it needs.
  • Google Business Profile: Google's page on managing Business Profile owners and managers says a profile "can have multiple owners but only one primary owner," and that managers have nearly the same access except that "they can't add or remove users or remove the profile." Keep primary ownership; the agency can work as a manager.
  • Domain, hosting and website: registered and billed in your name, with the agency given access.

For an initial audit, follow Google's own advice for SEO audits and "only grant read access to Search Console (at this stage, don't grant them write access)."

Term, cancellation and renewal

Check the minimum term, the notice period, whether the contract renews automatically and what fees apply on exit. A longer minimum can be reasonable for SEO, where work compounds over months, but it should come with milestones you can check along the way.

Deliverables and reporting

The scope should list outputs, not just effort: for example, the number of pages improved or published, campaigns built, tests run, and the report you receive each month with the KPIs and data sources agreed in Step 1. "Ongoing optimization" on its own is not a deliverable.

Ownership of work and exit handover

Content, creative, landing pages, tracking configuration and code you paid for should transfer to you on payment, and the contract should describe what the agency hands over when the relationship ends.

Agency Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some warning signs are matters of taste. These are not.

  • Guaranteed rankings. Google is blunt: "No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google." Its guidance tells you to "beware of SEOs that claim to guarantee rankings, allege a 'special relationship' with Google, or advertise a 'priority submit' to Google." Google also says that if an audit promises your changes will give you first place in search results, "find someone else."
  • Claims that ad spend helps organic rankings. Google states that "advertising with Google won't have any effect on your site's presence in our search results."
  • Unsolicited outreach with alarming claims. Google advises you to "be wary of SEO firms and web consultants or agencies that email you out of the blue."
  • Secrecy about methods. Google warns against a company that "is secretive or won't clearly explain what they intend to do," and reminds owners that "you are responsible for the actions of any companies you hire."
  • Offers to buy, write or "manage" reviews in ways the FTC rule prohibits, or audiences padded with fake followers.
  • Accounts in the agency's name, or refusal to give you admin access.
  • Link schemes or "thousands of directories." Google says to avoid SEOs that talk about link popularity schemes or submitting your site to thousands of search engines.

What the First 90 Days Should Look Like

A good start is visible before results are. In the first 30 days, expect access set up correctly, tracking checked, a baseline report and a prioritized plan. By day 60, the first high-impact changes should ship, such as landing page fixes, campaign restructuring or a conversion audit of the pages that get traffic but not leads. By day 90, leading indicators such as click-through, cost per lead and form completion rate should show a direction.

If, at 90 days, you cannot say what was done and why, raise it then, with the written scope and KPIs in hand. Our approach page shows how we structure this period.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose a digital marketing agency?

Define one outcome, KPI and budget first. Shortlist from referrals, ask three to five finalists the same questions, verify their proof, score them on one weighted scorecard, and sign only a contract that keeps your accounts in your name with clear deliverables and exit terms.

What questions should I ask a marketing agency?

Ask what they would do in the first 90 days, who will work on your account, how they measure success and from which data source, whose name the accounts will be in, and what happens to the work if you part ways.

What are the biggest red flags when hiring an agency?

Guaranteed rankings (Google says no one can guarantee a #1 ranking), claims of a special relationship with Google, secrecy about methods, accounts held in the agency's name, offers to buy or fake reviews, and proposals sent without any questions about your business.

Should the agency or my business own the Google Ads and GA4 accounts?

Your business should. Create the accounts under a company Google account, keep at least one admin login internally, and add the agency as a user. Google Ads, GA4 and Google Business Profile all support adding and removing users, so there is no technical reason for an agency to own them.

Is it better to hire an agency or build an in-house team?

An agency suits businesses that need several specialist skills or a fast start; an in-house hire suits constant, product-heavy work. Many growing businesses use a hybrid, with an internal marketer owning strategy and an agency handling specialist execution.

How are digital marketing agencies priced?

Common models are monthly retainers, project fees, performance-based fees, a percentage of ad spend and hourly billing. Ad spend is usually paid to the platform separately from the agency fee. Compare proposals in one format: fee, ad spend, software, setup, minimum term and monthly deliverables.
